Study 20 · Pyleecan, FEMM, CalculiX and Python

Traction motor, modelled from the client's data and checked against its test

The question

Can an open-source toolchain model a production traction motor well enough for design work? The motor is the 2004 Toyota Prius traction motor: 48 slots, 8 poles, magnets buried in the rotor in a V, rated 50 kW and 400 N m, 6,000 rpm maximum. Oak Ridge National Laboratory took one apart, drew it, measured its parts and tested it, and published all of it. I treat those reports the way I'd treat a client's data pack: the drawings, parts list, material data and routine tests are inputs, and the test results the model has to predict stay as the check.

The goals, set before any modelling:

  • Match the test. Back-EMF within 5 %, torque within 10 %, efficiency within 2 points.
  • Answer the design questions. If the inverter stops switching at top speed, does the voltage stay under the 500 V supply? Do the rotor's thin steel bridges stay elastic at 20 % overspeed? Does the rated 400 N m fit inside the copper's current limit? Does the motor hold 50 kW to 6,000 rpm, and 25 kW continuously from 1,250 rpm?

The answer so far

Back-EMF 17.7 % high, limit 5 %Torque 17 to 19 % high, limit 10 %Inductances within about 10 %Voltage at top speed, inverter off: 850 V measured, limit 500Rotor bridges past yield at overspeedEfficiency and power ratings: next

Not yet: the model reads about 18 % high, and I'm working out why. Built from the client's data with nothing tuned, it gives 105.2 V of back-EMF per 1,000 rpm where the test measured 89.4 V. Compared the way the test measures it, the locked-rotor torque is 17 to 19 % high at five of the seven test currents, 12 % at 125 A and 22 % at 250 A.

Both tests point to the same cause. Back-EMF, and torque at a given current, both scale with the flux the magnets drive through the winding. So the model carries about 18 % more magnet flux than the real motor. Every input a client supplies has been checked against the parts list and matches: the lamination geometry (confirmed by the stator core's mass), the winding, the magnets' size, mass and measured strength, and the test conditions. The cause is in something the 2D model takes for granted, not in the data.

Two design checks don't depend on it. If the inverter stops switching at 6,000 rpm, the spinning magnets generate far more than the 500 V supply: 850 V peak on the real motor, about 1,000 V in the model, so this check fails on the test data alone. And at 20 % overspeed the steel bridges that hold the magnets go past the yield of the stand-in steel.

What's next. One effect at a time, each with an estimate written before the run: the lamination steel in the bridges (running now, and my estimate says it moves the result by under 1 %), the end effects a 2D model leaves out (a published 3D study puts them at about 4 %, too small to be the whole answer), and a second, independent 2D model in ElmerFEM on the same geometry to check the field itself. The leading suspect is how fully the magnets are magnetised once they're in the rotor. That isn't something to fit: a simple test settles it, measuring one magnet's flux before and after re-magnetising it. The efficiency map and the power ratings all depend on the flux, so I'll run them once the 18 % is explained, rather than publish numbers I already know are off.

Why it matters

Most studies on this site check a model against theory: an exact solution, a handbook value, a conservation law. That shows the equations were solved correctly. It doesn't show the model matches a real machine, with real materials, tolerances and everything the model leaves out. Only measured data can show that, and it's what a client ultimately cares about.

This motor shows why. Every internal check on the model passes: the mesh studies, the power balance, the cross-checks between methods. Its inputs match the hardware. It still reads 18 % high against the test, and nothing short of the test would have shown it. Finding the cause, rather than tuning a number until the curves meet, is what makes the model usable for the next design.

Torque, compared like for like. The test locks the rotor, sets a fixed current and turns the shaft slowly, reporting the largest torque it sees. That includes the torque ripple, the small rise and fall as the magnets pass the slots. A model normally reports the average torque, which would put it only 4 % high at 50 A. So I reproduced the test from the model's saved torque at every rotor position: fixed current, the shaft turned, the largest value taken. Compared that way it's 17.5 % high at 50 A, the same as the back-EMF.

The air gap. The drawing gives 0.775 mm and the parts list 0.73 mm. Both are carried: the smaller gap reads one point higher, so it doesn't explain the difference.

Machine
The 2004 Toyota Prius traction motor: 48 stator slots, 8 poles, interior V-shaped permanent magnets, an 83.8 mm stack. Rated 400 N m and 50 kW, 6,000 rpm maximum.
Client data
Lamination geometry from the laboratory's dimensioned drawing, read from the vector outline in the PDF and not by eye. From the parts list: the winding (9 turns per coil, 8 coils in series per phase), the magnets (83.1 × 18.9 × 6.5 mm, 77 g each), the masses, a measured phase resistance of 69 mΩ at 21 °C, and an air gap of 0.73 mm against the drawing's 0.775. Assumed
The lamination grade isn't published, so M330-35A stands in, with other grades run to bracket it. The magnets are taken as fully magnetised, as the material sample was. Magnets at 25 °C, the temperature of the back-EMF test.
Electromagnetics
2D magnetostatics in FEMM 4.2, driven by Pyleecan, over one electrical period in 48 steps. Open circuit for the back-EMF; loaded runs sweep the current angle to find the most torque per amp; small current steps give the inductances.

How it works

The machine. Pyleecan, the open-source motor design library, ships a model of this motor. Rebuilt from the laboratory's own drawing, the real slot turns out to be 145 mm² and not 218, and the air gap 0.775 mm and not 0.750. So the study uses its own machine definition, and the figure below shows the difference.

Electromagnetics. FEMM solves the magnetic field in a cross-section of the motor at each rotor position. With no current, the changing flux through the windings gives the back-EMF, the voltage the spinning magnets generate. With current, the model sweeps the angle between the current and the magnets to find the most torque for each current level, and saves the torque at every rotor position, which is what lets it reproduce the locked-rotor test.

Rotor. The magnets sit in pockets, held in by thin bridges of steel at the rotor's edge. At speed, the magnets and the steel outside them pull outward on those bridges. The stress is linearised through each bridge, the same method as the pressure vessel in study 18, so the sharp pocket corners don't decide the answer.

How I checked it

  • The inputs against the hardware. The stator core computed from the drawing weighs 18.8 kg against 19.05 kg in the parts list (the mounting ears aren't modelled). The copper, with the modelled end-winding length, weighs 6.72 kg against 6.8. The magnet's size, volume and mass agree with each other at the density of sintered NdFeB.
  • Independent routes to the same number. A power balance (the electrical power going in against torque times speed) checks FEMM's torque to 0.1 to 0.4 %. Winding theory checks the flux linking the coils to 0.3 to 0.6 %. The open-circuit run passes all 10 of its checks and the torque run all 35.
  • A prediction before every run. The back-EMF was predicted from a magnetic-circuit estimate fitted to earlier runs of the same model, and came out within 0.1 V of it. So the model responds to the magnet's size and strength the way a simple magnetic circuit says it should: there's no numerical surprise inside it.
  • The rotor method on an exact case. A plain spinning disc, which has an exact solution, comes out right to 4 × 10⁻⁴ through the same pipeline.
  • Mesh studies on every quantity, with the coarsest mesh dropped where it left the thin bridges less than one element thick.
  • Sensitivity to what isn't published. Three lamination grades moved the back-EMF by 0.13 % and the torque by under 2 %, so the unknown steel grade can't explain 18 %. A run on the bridges' steel specifically is going now.

What I caught along the way

  • A library model that wasn't the motor it was named after. The library's slot is half as large again as the drawing's. The drawing itself turned out to be stretched 1.1 % in one direction, and was corrected before use.
  • A copper area 1.57 times too large. The laboratory quotes a slot fill of 0.84, and my first version applied it to the whole slot. Re-reading the definition, it's the fill of the squares around the insulated wires. The bare copper is about 0.59 of the slot, so the current limit is 212 A and not 333 A.
  • A computed resistance 14 % low. The winding's resistance worked out from the copper is 59.6 mΩ; the parts list gives 69 mΩ measured. Leads, joints and the real conductor length make a computed value low, so the measured one is used from here on.
  • Two tests that only looked inconsistent. Comparing the model's average torque with the test's largest torque made the motor look nearly right on torque and 18 % high on back-EMF, which can't both be true. Reproducing the test procedure from the saved torque made the two agree. Compare the statistic the test reports, not the one the model prints.
  • Three columns transcribed wrongly. In the laboratory's efficiency table, the winding temperature, phase voltage and phase current had been copied empty or shifted by one column in 345 of 428 rows. None had been used yet, and the columns the comparison uses were checked row by row against the table's own identities and were right.
  • An efficiency map that looked finished. The first map passed its spot checks but failed its interpolation check, all on one row of the table. At light load and high speed the map moved by up to 10 points with the table spacing. Finer rows at low current fixed it everywhere from 20 N m up, to under 1 point.
  • A hotspot in the wrong place. The thermal network first put the slot's hottest point in the middle of the winding and read 10 % low. A CalculiX model of the slot put it at the top, by the tooth tips. Reading the hotspot there halved the error.

Limits, and what I'd do next

  • The model's flux is about 18 % high until the cause is found, so its torque per amp is optimistic. That's why the efficiency map and the power ratings aren't on this page yet.
  • 2D magnetostatics: no end effects, no eddy currents in the magnets and no extra copper loss at high frequency. End effects are one of the effects being tested.
  • How fully the magnets are magnetised in the assembled rotor isn't in any of the reports. On a real job I'd ask the client's production engineer.
  • The lamination grade is a stand-in. Its effect on the back-EMF is bracketed; the overspeed verdict depends on its yield strength.
  • The laboratory also measured the air-gap field after taking the motor apart. A client wouldn't normally have that, so it isn't used.
