Flow Over a Backward-Facing Step, Against Measurements

The Question

Part 1 showed this setup gets the friction of attached flow right. Is it good enough when the flow separates and reattaches, for example to decide where a heat-transfer surface or a sensor can go in a duct with a step? The test is Driver and Seegmiller's backward-facing step: a 12.7 mm step in a wind tunnel, air at 44.2 m/s, a Reynolds number of 37,500 on the step height. Three limits were agreed before any of the comparison data was opened: where the flow reattaches, within 10 %; the skin friction on the floor from 10 to 30 step heights, within 15 % rms; and how far the pressure recovers by 30 step heights, within 0.02 in pressure coefficient.

The Answer

Reattachment 2.3 % long, limit 10 %Skin friction 7.0 % rms, limit 15 %Pressure recovery 0.023 high, limit 0.02

Two of three. The flow reattaches 6.40 step heights downstream against a measured 6.26, and the skin friction after it is 1 to 10 % low at every measured station, 7.0 % rms. Both pass. The pressure recovers to 0.153 against a measured 0.131, which misses the 0.02 limit by 0.003.

The miss has a likely cause, and it's one I'd stated before the comparison. The pressure peak behind reattachment is close (about 0.177 against 0.163). Further downstream the measured pressure falls back faster than mine. That's what the tunnel's side walls do: their boundary layers thicken, squeeze the core flow and lower the pressure. The tunnel is 12 step heights wide, and a 2D model has no side walls at all.

A hand estimate says the cause is big enough. I grew each side wall's boundary layer from my own 2D wall pressure. By 30 step heights downstream, the core runs 1.6 to 3.0 % faster and the pressure recovery drops by 0.028 to 0.052, depending on how thick the side walls' boundary layers are to begin with. Every case is at least as large as the miss. The estimate can't size the effect exactly, though; a 3D model with the side walls would.

Part 3 ran that 3D model, and the side walls are the cause. With them the pressure recovers to 0.132 against the measured 0.131, and reattachment and skin friction stay inside their limits. The 3D runs stopped short of their convergence target, though, so I report that result as unverified, not as a pass. A diagnostic rerun located the stall in a few cells just behind the step face, far from where the recovery is read.

Why It Matters

Most real ducts have steps, bends and sudden expansions, and the flow separates at them. Where it reattaches decides where heat transfer peaks and where a sensor would read sensibly. Turbulence models are known to struggle with separated flow, so a setup that passes on a straight pipe has to be tested again here, against measurements, before it's trusted.

The Model

Geometry
The experiment's channel: a 12.7 mm step, an inlet duct 8 step heights tall, a parallel top wall and an outlet 50 step heights downstream.

Air at a reference speed of 44.2 m/s, steady and incompressible. The inlet sits 1.15 m upstream, so both walls grow their own boundary layers, matched to the measured one 4 step heights before the step.

How I Checked It

  • The inflow first. The momentum thickness of the boundary layer arriving at the step matches the case description to 0.7 %. That's the input the separating flow inherits, and it was matched on what the description gives, not on the answer.
  • Mesh study. The reattachment length is 6.414, 6.408 and 6.404 step heights on the three meshes, a mesh uncertainty of 0.2 %. Skin friction at 20 step heights: 1.9 %. The pressure recovery moves by less than 0.001 between meshes, far less than the 0.003 it fails by.
  • Outlet length and inlet turbulence. Moving the outlet from 30 to 50 step heights changes the reattachment by one part in a million. Inlet turbulence from 0.5 to 2 % changes it by 0.06 %. Neither matters.
  • Scored once. The comparison ran a single time, from a script written after the predictions were committed.

What I Caught Along the Way

  • A finest mesh that didn't quite finish. It stopped at its 80,000-iteration cap just short of its velocity residual target. That's a verification fail on that mesh, and it's reported as one. Its effect on the verdicts is bounded by the spread between the meshes above.
  • A wrong prediction about where the friction would be low. I expected the model to read low just after reattachment, at 10 step heights. It reads low further on, from 16 to 28. It still passes, but the prediction was wrong and it's recorded as wrong.
  • A number I'd seen early, declared. The measured reattachment length was visible when the data was downloaded. It's declared in every case file, so that one result isn't blind. The friction and pressure tables stayed closed until the comparison.

Part 3: The Step in 3D, With the Tunnel's Side Walls

Part 2 missed the pressure recovery by 0.003 over its limit and put the miss down to the tunnel's side walls. Part 3 is the 3D model that tests that: the same step, the same three limits, and a side wall in the model. It covers half of the tunnel's 151 mm span, with a symmetry plane at mid-span, and every wall is resolved, the side wall included.

Pressure recovery 0.001 high, limit 0.02: unverifiedReattachment 2.3 % long, limit 10 %: unverifiedSkin friction 9.2 % rms, limit 15 %: unverified

All three limits are met on the numbers, but the result is unverified. The side walls explain Part 2's miss: on the same mesh the 2D model recovers 0.155 and the 3D model 0.132, against 0.131 measured. Reattachment hardly moves at mid-span, 6.40 step heights against 6.42 in 2D on the same mesh. The skin friction is 5 to 12 % low at the measured stations, 9.2 % rms. I don't count these as passes, because the 3D runs stopped short of their convergence target.

The one input the case description doesn't give is how thick the side walls' own boundary layers are at the step. The main case grows them from the same inlet as the floor's. A second case starts them 0.30 m before the step, which leaves them about a third as thick as the floor's. The recovery moves only from 0.132 to 0.135: a third of the layer still gives 88 % of the drop. With the thinner layers, reattachment is 2.8 % long and the skin friction 8.8 % rms, so both ends of the range meet the limits. A side-wall layer thicker than the floor's would lower the recovery further. I state that direction, but I didn't run it.

The runs stalled, and that's why the result is unverified. In every run with a side wall, the pressure residual stopped falling at about 2 × 10⁻⁵ after 2,000 iterations, some 20 times its target, and stayed there to the 12,000-iteration cap. The continuity error sat near 5 × 10⁻⁶ against a limit of 10⁻⁶. The numbers the verdicts use had settled: over the last 1,200 iterations the recovery moved by less than 10⁻⁶, and refining the mesh across the span changed it by 0.0004, against a margin of 0.019. So the stall is probably local.

The stall, located. A rerun to iteration 3,000 kept what the long runs hadn't: each cell's flux imbalance, the flow field at three late iterations, and probes read every iteration just under the step's lip. I wrote its predictions down first. It repeated the original run exactly; at iteration 3,000 the two logs agree to the last printed digit.

  • Where. Half the continuity error sits in 2,842 cells, 0.5 % of the mesh. Most of it is in the first two cell columns behind the step's vertical face, from the floor up to the lip, all across the span. Those are the cells where the exact-2D-limit run (below) failed its spanwise-velocity check.
  • What it is: a noise floor, not an unsteady feature of the flow. In those cells the flow barely moves late in the run, about 7 millionths of the reference speed per 100 iterations, and its direction reverses between successive intervals in 56 % of them, as random jitter would. The probe just under the lip shows the same: the spanwise velocity holds at 8 % of the reference speed and jitters with no period, each iteration carrying little memory of the last (correlation 0.06 to 0.23, depending on the trend removed). Where the flow still changes most, far downstream, it decays smoothly without reversing, to 0.91 of itself per 100 iterations over the whole domain.
  • Its effect. The recovery is read 4 step heights upstream and 30 downstream, far from those cells, and it had settled.

So the stall is located and its effect is bounded, but it isn't cleared. A failed residual check still means an unverified verdict, so the verdicts stay unverified. The one test left is the same run with Part 2's SIMPLE algorithm, which under-relaxes the pressure and may damp that jitter. I had predicted the stall would sit at the side wall, where the spanwise velocity is largest. It sat where the simpler limit case had already flagged a problem.

  • The exact 2D limit first. With the side wall made a symmetry plane, the flow is 2D and the 3D pipeline has to reproduce the 2D model on the same mesh. It does: reattachment to 8 × 10⁻⁵ and recovery to 2 × 10⁻⁵. That run failed two numerical checks of its own, a spanwise velocity of 0.13 % of the reference speed and a continuity error of 1.8 × 10⁻⁶, and it's recorded as a fail.
  • The inflow matched again. The side walls' boundary layers speed up the core flow, so the 3D inlet velocity was lowered to keep the stated speed 4 step heights before the step, within 0.2 %.
  • Mesh across the span. From 32 to 45 cells, the recovery changes by 0.0004 and the reattachment by 0.06 %. The mesh in the flow plane is one level, 17,700 cells, coarser than Part 2's: its 2D solution differs from Part 2's finest by 0.002 in recovery and 0.2 % in reattachment.
  • Predictions written first. Three held: the side walls lower the recovery by 0.018 to 0.035, the recovery then meets its limit, and reattachment stays close to the 2D value. Two didn't: I predicted every check would pass, and that thinner side-wall layers would give 60 to 85 % of the drop (88 %).

Limits, and What I'd Do Next

  • Part 2 is 2D, with no tunnel side walls. Part 3's 3D model shows they account for the pressure miss, but its runs stalled short of convergence, so that result is unverified. The stall is located, a noise floor in the first cells behind the step face, but not cleared; a rerun with Part 2's SIMPLE algorithm is the one test left.
  • Part 3 models half the span with a symmetry plane, on one mesh in the flow plane. The side walls' boundary layers at the step aren't given in the case description, so they're bracketed, on the thin side only.
  • One geometry, one Reynolds number and one turbulence model. Steady flow, so the shear layer's unsteadiness isn't resolved.
  • The model's boundary layer is fuller than the measured one at the same momentum thickness. The reattachment was insensitive to that in the smoke tests, but it's a stated difference.
