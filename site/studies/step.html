Flow Over a Backward-Facing Step, Against Measurements

The Question

Part 1 showed this setup gets the friction of attached flow right. Is it good enough when the flow separates and reattaches, for example to decide where a heat-transfer surface or a sensor can go in a duct with a step? The test is Driver and Seegmiller's backward-facing step: a 12.7 mm step in a wind tunnel, air at 44.2 m/s, a Reynolds number of 37,500 on the step height. Three limits were agreed before any of the comparison data was opened: where the flow reattaches, within 10 %; the skin friction on the floor from 10 to 30 step heights, within 15 % rms; and how far the pressure recovers by 30 step heights, within 0.02 in pressure coefficient.

The Answer

Reattachment 2.3 % long, limit 10 %Skin friction 7.0 % rms, limit 15 %Pressure recovery 0.023 high, limit 0.02

Two of three. The flow reattaches 6.40 step heights downstream against a measured 6.26, and the skin friction after it is 1 to 10 % low at every measured station, 7.0 % rms. Both pass. The pressure recovers to 0.153 against a measured 0.131, which misses the 0.02 limit by 0.003.

The miss has a likely cause, and it's one I'd stated before the comparison. The pressure peak behind reattachment is close (about 0.177 against 0.163). Further downstream the measured pressure falls back faster than mine. That's what the tunnel's side walls do: their boundary layers thicken, squeeze the core flow and lower the pressure. The tunnel is 12 step heights wide, and a 2D model has no side walls at all.

A hand estimate says the cause is big enough. I grew each side wall's boundary layer from my own 2D wall pressure. By 30 step heights downstream, the core runs 1.6 to 3.0 % faster and the pressure recovery drops by 0.028 to 0.052, depending on how thick the side walls' boundary layers are to begin with. Every case is at least as large as the miss. The estimate can't size the effect exactly, though; a 3D model with the side walls would.

Why It Matters

Most real ducts have steps, bends and sudden expansions, and the flow separates at them. Where it reattaches decides where heat transfer peaks and where a sensor would read sensibly. Turbulence models are known to struggle with separated flow, so a setup that passes on a straight pipe has to be tested again here, against measurements, before it's trusted.

The Model

Geometry
The experiment's channel: a 12.7 mm step, an inlet duct 8 step heights tall, a parallel top wall and an outlet 50 step heights downstream.
Flow
Air at a reference speed of 44.2 m/s, steady and incompressible. The inlet sits 1.15 m upstream, so both walls grow their own boundary layers, matched to the measured one 4 step heights before the step.
Turbulence
k-omega SST, the same setup as part 1, with the thin layer at every wall resolved.
Mesh
About 36,000, 71,000 and 142,000 cells for the mesh study.
What's compared
Reattachment length, skin friction on the floor and the pressure recovery, against the published tables for the parallel-wall case.

How I Checked It

  • The inflow first. The momentum thickness of the boundary layer arriving at the step matches the case description to 0.7 %. That's the input the separating flow inherits, and it was matched on what the description gives, not on the answer.
  • Mesh study. The reattachment length is 6.414, 6.408 and 6.404 step heights on the three meshes, a mesh uncertainty of 0.2 %. Skin friction at 20 step heights: 1.9 %. The pressure recovery moves by less than 0.001 between meshes, far less than the 0.003 it fails by.
  • Outlet length and inlet turbulence. Moving the outlet from 30 to 50 step heights changes the reattachment by one part in a million. Inlet turbulence from 0.5 to 2 % changes it by 0.06 %. Neither matters.
  • Scored once. The comparison ran a single time, from a script written after the predictions were committed.

What I Caught Along the Way

  • A finest mesh that didn't quite finish. It stopped at its 80,000-iteration cap just short of its velocity residual target. That's a verification fail on that mesh, and it's reported as one. Its effect on the verdicts is bounded by the spread between the meshes above.
  • A wrong prediction about where the friction would be low. I expected the model to read low just after reattachment, at 10 step heights. It reads low further on, from 16 to 28. It still passes, but the prediction was wrong and it's recorded as wrong.
  • A number I'd seen early, declared. The measured reattachment length was visible when the data was downloaded. It's declared in every case file, so that one result isn't blind. The friction and pressure tables stayed closed until the comparison.

Limits, and What I'd Do Next

  • 2D: no tunnel side walls, which is the likely cause of the pressure miss. A hand estimate of their blockage is big enough to explain it; a 3D model of the full span would size it.
  • One geometry, one Reynolds number and one turbulence model. Steady flow, so the shear layer's unsteadiness isn't resolved.
  • The model's boundary layer is fuller than the measured one at the same momentum thickness. The reattachment was insensitive to that in the smoke tests, but it's a stated difference.
