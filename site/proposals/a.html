What I Can Take On, and What You Get

The deliverable is a memo a design lead can act on, with every number traceable to a saved run.

  • Build and check a modelFrom your drawings and requirements to a verdict on each requirement, with the model, the independent check behind it and the files to repeat it.
  • Check a result you already haveAn independent second answer to a number someone else produced: a hand calculation, a second tool or a mesh study, and a plain statement of what holds.
  • Automate a repeated analysisA sweep or an optimisation loop that an AI agent runs under written procedures, with the same checks on every run.
  • Engineering tasks for AI trainingRequirement-to-verdict tasks with automated graders, like the ones on the grading page.
  1. Set upTurn the question into requirements I can measure, along with the input ranges, the assumptions that need checking and the acceptance tolerances. All of it goes in a case file.
  2. BuildWrite the geometry, mesh, materials, loads and solver inputs as code, using CadQuery, Gmsh, CalculiX, OpenFOAM, SciPy or Modelica.
  3. CheckCompare every number I report with something independent. That might be an exact solution, a conservation law, a handbook value or a second tool, plus a mesh or time-step convergence study.
  4. ReportLink each requirement to its result, the checks behind it and the saved files. If an assumption broke along the way, I hold the verdict back instead of guessing.

Cold-Plate Coolant Passage, Pressure Budget and Wall Shear

Subject
Does the 1 mm coolant passage meet its pressure budget and minimum wall shear across tolerance and temperature?
Status
Analysis complete; recommendation pending your decision on the requirements

The passage as specified does not meet its requirements, and no choice of gap alone can fix it.

  • At nominal conditions (1.00 mm gap, 20 °C) the passage needs 33.4 Pa of supply pressure against a 30 Pa budget, 11 % over.
  • Across the gap tolerance (±0.05 mm) and coolant range (15–40 °C) it needs 28–42 Pa at the four verified corners, and warm coolant drops the wall shear to 0.43 Pa against a 0.5 Pa minimum (0.36 Pa at the warm, wide-gap corner, which is unverified; see below).

Sheet 4

What the Checks Caught

In 8 studies my first model gave an answer that looked reasonable and was wrong. Four of them:

  • Bracket optimisation · design automationTwo answers that failed their own checksThe optimiser's first pick read 94 MPa, but its mesh study put it at 101.7 MPa once the uncertainty was added: a peak in a fillet needs far more margin than a smooth bending stress. The second pick failed because the mesh study wasn't refining the fillet at all. The design on the page passed only once the peak itself was refined.
  • Pressure vessel · design codeA pass that depended on where one line was drawnRead on lines at the weld toes, the nozzle passed its design-code limit with 0.9 % to spare, and I recommended a wall on that basis. Measuring the other usual placement, a line through the inner corner, gave 25 % more stress on the same converged model. The verdict flipped and the recommendation was withdrawn.
  • Traction motor · electric machinesA library model that wasn't the motor it was named afterThe open-source motor library ships a model of the 2004 Prius motor, so the obvious start was to use it. Rebuilt from the laboratory's dimensioned drawing, the real slot is 145 mm², not 218, and the air gap 0.775 mm, not 0.750. The drawing itself turned out to be stretched 1.1 % in one direction. The study uses its own machine definition, checked against the parts list's masses.
  • Strap · stress concentrationA model that converged neatly to the wrong answerThe mesh study looked textbook, but it settled 2 % away from the handbook value. Running a case with a known exact answer through the same pipeline showed the problem was in how the solver's elements behaved, which no amount of mesh refinement would have revealed.
